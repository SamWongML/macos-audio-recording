# Accessibility adaptation on macOS 26/27: what a genuinely modern app owes, and what AppTape should build

Research for [#74](https://github.com/SamWongML/macos-audio-recording/issues/74) ("Decide AppTape's design language"), part of the map in [#70](https://github.com/SamWongML/macos-audio-recording/issues/70). Answers: what does a genuinely modern macOS 26/27 app do about accessibility display settings, and what is the optimal, concrete rule set for AppTape's editor — given the design language already locked (one committed indigo accent, "Signal," on content only; Liquid Glass on exactly two controls; standard materials everywhere else) and the standing amber-is-colour-only trade in [ADR-0009](../adr/0009-the-disk-guard-is-a-runway-clock.md).

## Method and sourcing

Two research passes: (1) first-party Apple documentation — the HIG, SwiftUI/AppKit API reference, and the `Accessibility` framework's own testing guide, via `mcp__xcode__DocumentationSearch` and direct fetches; (2) the actual WWDC25 "Meet Liquid Glass" session transcript, plus community reporting on macOS Tahoe's shipped behavior, fetched directly. Every claim below carries its source URL inline. Two spots in AppTape's *current, checked-in* Swift source were read directly and are cited by file and function name, not guessed at.

**Sourcing caveats, stated up front rather than buried:**

- **The single most load-bearing fact in this research — whether Liquid Glass self-adapts to Reduce Transparency/Increase Contrast/Reduce Motion with zero app code — is settled by a direct quote from the WWDC25 "Meet Liquid Glass" session transcript** (fetched from `https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/`), corroborated by the HIG's own Materials page. This is as solid as sourcing gets for a behavioral claim about a system API — but it describes the *contract*, not every shipped build's fidelity to it (see the Tahoe precedent in §7).
- **No screenshots were captured.** Every claim about visual appearance rests on written sources — Apple's own docs, the WWDC transcript, and community write-ups (Pixel Envy, OS X Daily, Six Colors) that describe what they saw. Nothing here was independently verified against a running build by this research.
- **AppTape's Trim region, playhead, and Signal-coloured waveform do not exist in the codebase yet.** The design decisions in the prompt (one indigo accent named Signal, content-only; two Liquid Glass controls) are the *not-yet-written* outcome #74 is deciding, not code I read. Where this document makes a recommendation about "the playhead" or "the Trim region," it is forward-looking guidance for when that code is written, grounded in Apple's documented behaviour — not a description of something that exists today.
- **Two findings below *are* drawn from AppTape's real, current source** (`AppTape/AppTape/ExportInspector.swift`'s `presetRow`, and `AppTape/AppTape/EditorView.swift`'s `LibraryRow`), read directly in this session. These are flagged as verified-against-code, not inferred.
- **Bold Text's status on macOS is a genuine, slightly surprising finding** — see §5 — resting on the structure of Apple's own testing-guide page plus corroborating community reporting, since Apple does not appear to have written a single sentence anywhere stating "macOS does not have Bold Text."
- A few claims (SF Symbols' indefinite effects auto-honouring Reduce Motion; the exact CSS-like mechanics of `NSVisualEffectView`'s Reduce-Transparency swap) rest on secondary/community sources because no primary Apple doc page stated them in so many words in what this research could find. These are flagged inline and again in "what I could not verify."

## 1. Reduce Transparency and Liquid Glass

### The load-bearing fact, nailed down

**Yes — settled, from Apple's own mouth.** The WWDC25 session "Meet Liquid Glass" states plainly, of Reduced Transparency, Increased Contrast, and Reduced Motion together:

> "These act as modifiers on the material that change certain layers of Liquid Glass, without sacrificing its magic. For instance, Reduced Transparency, makes Liquid Glass frostier and obscures more of the content behind it. Increased contrast, makes elements predominantly black or white and highlights them with a contrasting border and Reduced Motion decreases the intensity of some effects and disables any elastic properties for the material. **These are available automatically whenever you use the new material. So whenever these settings are turned on at a system-level, Liquid Glass elements will get them across the board.**"
> — [Meet Liquid Glass, WWDC25](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/) (transcript, emphasis added)

The HIG's own Materials page corroborates this without contradiction: "The appearance of these variants [`.regular` and `.clear`] can differ in response to certain system settings, like if people choose a preferred look for Liquid Glass in their device's settings, or turn on accessibility settings that reduce transparency or increase contrast in the interface." — [Materials: Liquid Glass](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/materials#Liquid-Glass)

**What this means concretely for AppTape:** `.glassEffect(...)`, `.buttonStyle(.glass)`, and `.buttonStyle(.glassProminent)` all construct the same underlying `Glass` structure ([`Glass`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/SwiftUI/Glass), [`glassEffect(_:in:)`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/SwiftUI/View/glassEffect(_:in:)), [`GlassButtonStyle`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/SwiftUI/GlassButtonStyle), [`GlassProminentButtonStyle`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/SwiftUI/GlassProminentButtonStyle)) whether a system control uses it or a custom view does. The WWDC statement is unqualified — it does not say "system components only" — so both of AppTape's two glass controls (the transport play/pause button and the Export button) get the frostier/opaque substitution, the black-or-white-with-border Increase Contrast treatment, and the de-elasticized Reduce Motion treatment **without any app code**, precisely because they use `.buttonStyle(.glass)` / `.buttonStyle(.glassProminent)` rather than a hand-drawn approximation.

One caveat on precision: neither the transcript nor the HIG describes the literal rendered result in pixel terms ("renders as an opaque `NSColor.windowBackgroundColor` fill," say). "Frostier and obscures more of the content behind it" is qualitative language, not an implementation spec. Apple has deliberately kept this as a material-level contract, not a value the app can read and imitate — there is no public API that hands back "what Reduce-Transparency-adjusted Liquid Glass looks like" as a color. That is fine for AppTape's purposes (nothing needs to imitate it), but it is worth stating plainly that the *behavior* is confirmed, the *exact rendering* is not documented to the pixel.

### Standard materials (`.regularMaterial`, `.ultraThinMaterial`, etc.)

These are the *other* system-provided translucency mechanism — SwiftUI's `Material` type (backed by `NSVisualEffectView` on macOS), used for the content layer rather than the functional/chrome layer ([Materials: Liquid Glass](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/materials#Liquid-Glass) explicitly separates the two: "Instead, use Standard materials for elements in the content layer"). Apple's own testing guide for accessibility features shows exactly this working automatically for system-provided chrome, with a before/after screenshot pair captioned: "**Reduce transparency**. This option toggles the Reduce Transparency setting, which improves contrast and legibility by reducing transparency and blur effects on certain backgrounds," showing a menu whose "background material peek[ing] through" becomes "menu items that have an opaque background." — [Testing system accessibility features in your app](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/Accessibility/testing-system-accessibility-features-in-your-app#All-platforms)

This is Apple's own system chrome (menus), not proof that a third-party app's `.background(.regularMaterial)` gets the same free ride. I could not find a single Apple documentation sentence stating "SwiftUI's `Material` type automatically substitutes an opaque fill under Reduce Transparency" in so many words. What I did confirm from a primary source is the older, underlying mechanism: AppKit's `NSVisualEffectView` — which `Material` is built on — has shipped since 10.10 with the `accessibilityDisplayShouldReduceTransparency` contract as an *app-observable* signal (`NSWorkspace.accessibilityDisplayShouldReduceTransparency`: "If this property is `true`, don't use semitransparent backgrounds in the user interface. For example, use only opaque windows." — [`accessibilityDisplayShouldReduceTransparency`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/AppKit/NSWorkspace/accessibilityDisplayShouldReduceTransparency)) — worded as **guidance to the app**, not as "the system already handled it for you." Community-sourced but consistent secondary reporting (multiple independent write-ups, not one source) states that `NSVisualEffectView`/`Material` blur *does* auto-degrade to a solid fill under Reduce Transparency at the system compositing level. Given the wording difference between the Liquid Glass transcript ("available automatically... across the board") and the `NSWorkspace` property doc ("don't use... " — imperative, addressed to the developer), **the honest, conservative answer is: Liquid Glass's automatic behavior is confirmed by Apple in its own words; standard `Material`'s automatic behavior is well-corroborated but not found stated by Apple with the same unambiguous "you don't need to do anything" phrasing.** AppTape does not have to resolve this ambiguity in practice, since the map already commits standard materials to chrome only and nothing in AppTape's chrome plan uses a hand-rolled blur — but an ADR should not claim more certainty here than the sourcing supports.

### Pattern

The system's own contract is generous — for the two controls that carry actual Liquid Glass, Reduce Transparency (and Increase Contrast, and Reduce Motion) really is free. The discipline this buys AppTape: *do not* build a third, hand-rolled "glass-like" control anywhere else in the editor, because a hand-rolled approximation gets none of this — only genuine `.glassEffect`/`.buttonStyle(.glass...)` usage is covered by the WWDC guarantee.

## 2. Reduce Motion

### What SwiftUI does automatically, and what remains the app's job

**Two different things are being asked here, and they get different answers.** Liquid Glass's own *intrinsic* physics — the material's elasticity, its bounce, its morph/lensing behavior during a `GlassEffectContainer` transition — get the same "automatic... across the board" treatment quoted in §1: "Reduced Motion decreases the intensity of some effects and disables any elastic properties for the material." — [Meet Liquid Glass, WWDC25](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/219/). This covers, specifically, the [`GlassEffectTransition.matchedGeometry`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/SwiftUI/GlassEffectTransition/matchedGeometry) morph AppTape's transport play/pause button and Export button could in principle use if they ever moved or merged into one another — the elastic overshoot on that morph is suppressed by the system, not by AppTape.

**Everything else — any `.animation(_:value:)`, `withAnimation { }`, or custom transition the app itself writes — is entirely the app's responsibility.** Apple's own visionOS accessibility guide states this without qualification: "The Reduce Motion system setting lets you know when to provide alternatives for all of your app's motion effects... When the setting is `true`, provide suitable alternatives for motion effects or eliminate them altogether." — [Improving accessibility support in your visionOS app](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/visionOS/improving-accessibility-support-in-your-app#Limit-motion-effects-in-your-content), citing the same [`accessibilityReduceMotion`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/SwiftUI/EnvironmentValues/accessibilityReduceMotion) environment value that exists on macOS. Community documentation converges on the same reading for the ordinary `withAnimation`/`.animation` call: SwiftUI does not gate it on the environment value for you; the app must read `@Environment(\.accessibilityReduceMotion)` and branch. This directly bears on AppTape's own code: **`ExportInspector.swift` currently has four unconditional `.animation(.default, value:)` calls** (on `recording.trimmedDuration`, `coordinator.phase`, `preference.normalizeLoudness`, and `editor.correction.state`) that will keep animating at full strength under Reduce Motion unless the ADR adds a rule for them.

### The correct replacement — Apple's stated preference

Apple's HIG states this plainly, as one of a named list of "best practices" for reducing motion, under Accessibility: Cognitive:

> "Other best practices for reducing motion include: Tightening animation springs to reduce bounce effects; Tracking animations directly with people's gestures; Avoiding animating depth changes in z-axis layers; **Replacing transitions in x-, y-, and z-axes with fades to avoid motion**; Avoiding animating into and out of blurs."
> — [Accessibility: Cognitive](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility#Cognitive)

This is a direct, primary-source answer to "instant cut vs. cross-fade vs. opacity-only": **Apple's own stated preference is a fade (an opacity-based cross-fade), not an instant cut.** A cut is not called out anywhere in the HIG as the preferred substitute; it is one acceptable degenerate case of "eliminate the motion effect" from the visionOS guide's "provide suitable alternatives... or eliminate them altogether" language, but the HIG's own itemized list names fades specifically. iOS/iPadOS additionally exposes a distinct, named "Prefer Cross-Fade Transitions" setting layered on top of Reduce Motion (`UIAccessibility.prefersCrossFadeTransitions`, [docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/UIKit/UIAccessibility/prefersCrossFadeTransitions)) — this is a UIKit/iOS-specific API with no found AppKit/macOS equivalent, so it should be read as *reinforcing* evidence that fades are Apple's house style for a suppressed transition, not as something AppTape can call directly on macOS.

### Nothing macOS-26/27-specific was found about Liquid Glass morph/lensing beyond the WWDC quote above

I looked for a dedicated statement about the *lensing* effect (the refraction/distortion Liquid Glass shows near its edges) under Reduce Motion specifically, distinct from the general "elastic properties" sentence, and did not find one. Treat the WWDC quote as covering both morph and lensing under the umbrella "some effects... intensity decreases," since Apple did not further subdivide the material's behaviors in the transcript found.

### Pattern

The rule that emerges, cleanly: **Liquid Glass's own material behavior is a freebie; anything the app itself animates is not.** AppTape's two glass controls need zero Reduce Motion code. Every other transition the editor will write — the Trim handle drag settling, the playhead's seek animation, the four existing `.animation(.default, value:)` calls in `ExportInspector.swift` — needs an explicit `accessibilityReduceMotion` branch, and Apple's own preference for what that branch should produce is a fade, not a cut.

## 3. Differentiate Without Color

### Does macOS expose this, in both forms asked about?

**Yes, confirmed as both an environment value and an AppKit property.** SwiftUI: `accessibilityDifferentiateWithoutColor` — "Whether the system preference for Differentiate without Color is enabled... If this is true, UI should not convey information using color alone and instead should use shapes or glyphs to convey information." — [`accessibilityDifferentiateWithoutColor`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/SwiftUI/EnvironmentValues/accessibilityDifferentiateWithoutColor). AppKit: `NSWorkspace.accessibilityDisplayShouldDifferentiateWithoutColor` — "A Boolean value that indicates whether the app avoids conveying information through color alone." — [`NSWorkspace: Supporting Accessibility`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/AppKit/NSWorkspace#Supporting-Accessibility). `NSWorkspace` is an AppKit-only, Mac-only type, which by itself confirms macOS support independent of the SwiftUI value.

### The correct response, and the ADR-0009 assessment

The HIG states the underlying principle unconditionally, not as something contingent on the setting being on: **"Convey information with more than color alone."** — "Offer visual indicators, like distinct shapes or icons, in addition to color to help people perceive differences in function and changes in state." — [Accessibility: Vision](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility#Vision). The parallel HIG Color page states the identical rule from the other direction: "Avoid relying solely on color to differentiate between objects, indicate interactivity, or communicate essential information. When you use color to convey information, be sure to provide the same information in alternative ways." — [Color: Inclusive color](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/color#Inclusive-color).

This phrasing matters for judging ADR-0009 precisely: **Apple's stated rule is unconditional — "always," not "when the Differentiate-Without-Color toggle is on."** By the letter of that guidance, the amber-only 3-hour Runway tier (colour, no glyph, no words, by ADR-0009's own account) is a genuine gap against the HIG's default expectation, not merely against an opt-in accommodation. That said, the HIG is advisory, not a pass/fail gate, and ADR-0009 already frames this as a "conscious accessibility trade" with two real mitigations baked into the design itself, not asserted after the fact: (1) the harm is bounded to an *early, non-blocking* advisory — a healthy disk never shows amber at all, and the Recording keeps running regardless of whether the color registers; (2) the same information is guaranteed to reach the same user, in text, 2.5 hours later at the 30-minute tier, before anything is actually lost. That two-channel, escalating design is itself consistent with Apple's own general feedback guidance to use multiple channels rather than one ("When you use multiple ways to provide feedback, you reach more people" — [Feedback: Best practices](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/feedback#Best-practices)) — it just places the *redundant* channel at the second tier rather than the first.

**Verdict: defensible as a bounded, disclosed engineering trade-off; not fully compliant with the HIG's own unconditional wording.** It is not the kind of thing Apple's App Store review is likely to flag (it is one status-item tier of one small utility, not a core workflow), but it is also not something the ADR should describe as "meets the guideline" — it should be described exactly as ADR-0009 already describes it: a trade, consciously made, with a stated boundary on how bad it can get.

Apple does have a formal, App-Store-facing mechanism for exactly this axis — self-declared **Accessibility Nutrition Labels**, which include a "Differentiate Without Relying on Color" category app developers can declare support for. — [Accessibility Nutrition Labels overview](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-app-accessibility/overview-of-accessibility-nutrition-labels) (referenced from [HIG: Accessibility](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)). If AppTape ever wants to claim that label truthfully, the amber-only tier is the one thing standing in the way — worth naming in the ADR as the reason not to claim it yet, rather than silently ignoring the mismatch.

### Does the answer change with the indigo Signal fill?

**Not because of any interaction between the two colors — because it raises the bar.** Signal (editor content) and amber (menu bar Runway advisory) live in entirely separate surfaces that are never visible together, so there is no risk of a user literally confusing one hue for the other in the same view. But an app that now commits real visual weight to color-as-meaning in one place (the waveform, Trim, playhead, selected preset) has a harder time arguing that color-only signaling is a *minor, out-of-scope* concern elsewhere — the app's own design language has just demonstrated it takes color seriously enough to build a whole accent around it. This is my own reading of the trade-off, not a documented Apple position: Apple's guidance doesn't grade apps on "how much color you use elsewhere," only on whether each specific signal is color-only. I'll also flag, labelled explicitly as my own observation rather than sourced Apple guidance: indigo (Signal) and amber (Runway) sit closer to the blue–yellow confusion axis associated with the rarer *tritanopia* form of color blindness than to the far more common red–green axis — low real-world risk given the two never appear together, but worth naming since it wasn't obvious from the color choices alone.

**A concrete, code-verified place where the answer *does* change, found in AppTape's actual current source:** `ExportInspector.swift`'s `presetRow` — the exact view the design decision names as one of Signal's four intended homes ("the selected export-preset rung") — currently marks the selected Quality Preset **only** by (a) a `.tint.opacity(0.14)` background fill versus `.clear`, and (b) the size-estimate text rendered in `.tint` versus `.secondary`. There is no checkmark, no border, no bold weight, no text difference between a selected and unselected row beyond those two color/opacity cues. Once this row is repainted in Signal indigo instead of the system tint, it becomes the one place in the whole design where Signal genuinely *is* the sole differentiator of a piece of information (which preset is selected) — exactly the pattern Apple's guidance calls out. This is not hypothetical; it is the present state of the code, and it is a concrete, actionable finding for the ADR (see §6).

## 4. Increase Contrast

### The interaction with a custom accent, and best practice

SwiftUI exposes the setting as `colorSchemeContrast` ([`ColorSchemeContrast`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/SwiftUI/ColorSchemeContrast)): "You receive a contrast value when you read the `colorSchemeContrast` environment value... The user sets the contrast by selecting the Increase Contrast option in Accessibility > Display in System Preferences on macOS... **Your app can't override the user's choice.**" This is a read-only signal, not a control — the app can only *respond*, via `.standard`/`.increased` cases, never force a value. AppKit exposes the equivalent as `NSWorkspace.accessibilityDisplayShouldIncreaseContrast` — [`NSWorkspace: Supporting Accessibility`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/AppKit/NSWorkspace#Supporting-Accessibility).

**Does an app need to ship a high-contrast variant of a custom named accent in the asset catalog, and is it best practice?** Yes on both counts, and this is a documented, Xcode-native mechanism, not a workaround: a Color Set asset supports "Any," "Dark," and — orthogonally — a "High Contrast" checkbox under Appearances in Xcode's Attributes inspector, giving up to four stored variants of one named color (Any, Any-High-Contrast, Dark, Dark-High-Contrast). Apple's own Dark Mode guidance folds this into the same sentence as the ordinary light/dark variant: "When you need a custom color, add a Color Set asset to your app's asset catalog in Xcode, and specify the bright and dim variants of the color... You can also specify high-contrast versions of your colors." — [Supporting Dark Mode in your interface: Choose adaptive colors for your UI](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/UIKit/supporting-dark-mode-in-your-interface#Choose-adaptive-colors-for-your-UI) (a UIKit-titled page, but the asset-catalog mechanism it describes is the same one AppKit/`NSColor(named:)` and SwiftUI's `Color("Signal")` all read from). The analogous, explicitly-macOS-facing statement for images makes the same point about the underlying asset-catalog contract: "High contrast. Provide images with greater visual separation between adjoining colors. Such images make it easier for people with vision impairments to see the image details." — [Providing images for different appearances](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/UIKit/providing-images-for-different-appearances#Customize-image-assets-for-different-appearances). A practical secondary source walks through the exact Xcode steps and states the recommendation for a branded accent directly: "If you use a custom color, supply light and dark variants, and an increased contrast option for each variant... it's recommended to have text colors, tint colors, and any 'accent' colors respond to Increase Contrast." — [Supporting Increase Contrast in your app to enhance Accessibility](https://www.createwithswift.com/supporting-increase-contrast-in-your-app-to-enhance-accessibility/).

**For AppTape specifically: yes, ship a High Contrast variant of Signal.** It is a committed, deliberately visible brand accent that carries real meaning (the waveform body, the Trim region, the playhead, the selected preset) — precisely the category this guidance is aimed at, not an incidental decoration. This costs one extra color definition per appearance in the asset catalog, with no code change needed elsewhere, since `Color("Signal")` / `NSColor(named:)` already re-resolves automatically whenever the environment changes.

### A concrete platform quirk worth building around

On shipped macOS Tahoe, Increase Contrast and Reduce Transparency are not fully independent settings in practice: "In Tahoe, enabling Increase Contrast also forces Reduce Transparency to turn on and locks it in that state." — [Six Colors: Soaping up Liquid Glass](https://sixcolors.com/post/2025/11/soaping-up-liquid-glass-less-transparency-more-contrast/). AppTape's own code will still read the two environment values independently (they remain logically distinct booleans in SwiftUI), but this real-world coupling means any manual test pass only needs three states, not four, to exercise the practical combinations users will actually have: neither on, Reduce Transparency alone, and both together.

Directly touches four existing `.animation(.default, value:)` calls in `ExportInspector.swift` today; not optional once the app animates anything beyond the two glass controls. |
| **Reduce Transparency** | Yes — see §1. | Expected, and already partly done: the panel already swaps to `Color(nsColor: .windowBackgroundColor)`. The editor currently has "almost no blur," per the prompt, so the practical surface area to cover is small — mostly a matter of not introducing new hand-rolled translucency outside the two glass controls. |
| **Increase Contrast** | Yes — `colorSchemeContrast` / `NSWorkspace.accessibilityDisplayShouldIncreaseContrast`. | **Differentiate Without Color** | Yes — see §3. | Expected, and cheap: AppTape's four Signal use-sites are already three-for-four structurally redundant with shape/position (waveform shape, Trim handle position, playhead position); the one real gap (`presetRow`'s selection state) is a small, targeted fix, not an app-wide audit. |
| **Invert Colors** | Yes — `accessibilityInvertColors` (SwiftUI) / `NSWorkspace.accessibilityDisplayShouldInvertColors` (AppKit); the opt-out hook is [`accessibilityIgnoresInvertColors(_:)`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/SwiftUI/View/accessibilityIgnoresInvertColors(_:)). | **Over-engineering to build for explicitly.** The opt-out modifier exists specifically for content where inversion actively breaks meaning — photos, video, rich media (["Sets whether this view should ignore the system Smart Invert setting"](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/SwiftUI/View-Accessibility#Color-inversion)). AppTape's waveform is a data chart, not a photograph — an inverted indigo waveform is unusual-looking but not unreadable. No action recommended beyond letting the system do what it already does; **could not verify** whether macOS's "Invert colors" performs the same per-view-exemptible Smart Invert as iOS, or a blunter whole-display inversion outside any app's control — flagged in §8. |
| **Bold Text** | **No, not really — a genuine, specific finding.** Apple's own testing guide places "Bold fonts" under a section header scoped to **"iOS, watchOS, and tvOS"** specifically ([Testing system accessibility features in your app](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/Accessibility/testing-system-accessibility-features-in-your-app#iOS-watchOS-and-tvOS-3)) — it is conspicuously *not* listed under the page's separate "All platforms" section that Differentiate Without Color, Invert Colors, and Increase/Reduce settings all appear under. `UIAccessibility.isBoldTextEnabled` is a UIKit-only symbol; there is no macOS `NSWorkspace` counterpart. Community reporting on macOS Tahoe corroborates this from the user side: searching "Bold" in System Settings or Spotlight in Tahoe 26.2 "returns nothing," and the underlying `EnhancedTextLegibilityEnabled` default exists only as an undocumented Terminal-only key, never surfaced as a System Settings toggle. SwiftUI's `legibilityWeight` environment value ([`LegibilityWeight`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/SwiftUI/LegibilityWeight)) is technically cross-platform API, but with no mainstream way for a Mac user to ever set it to `.bold`. | **Not actionable, and not the app's gap to close.** No code recommended; noting it exists as an environment value costs nothing (SF's own weight table already gives good legibility per the [Typography](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography#Ensuring-legibility) guidance the premium-treatment research already covered), but building a bespoke bold-text mode for a setting macOS doesn't expose to users would be pure over-engineering. |
| **Sidebar icon size** | Yes — `sidebarRowSize` environment value: "On macOS, reflects the value of the 'Sidebar icon size' in System Settings' Appearance settings... SwiftUI views like `Label` automatically adapt to the sidebar row size." — [`sidebarRowSize`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/SwiftUI/EnvironmentValues/sidebarRowSize). Also documented in the HIG: "A sidebar's row height, text, and glyph size depend on its overall size, which can be small, medium, or large... people can also change it by selecting a different sidebar icon size in General settings." — [Sidebars: macOS](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sidebars#macOS). | **A genuine, verified gap in AppTape's current code — but a density preference, not a disability accommodation, so it is low priority, not urgent.** `EditorView.swift`'s `LibraryRow` is a hand-composed `HStack` with a hardcoded `.frame(height: 32)`, not a `Label`-based row — it will not respond to this setting at all today. The row's fixed height is itself a *deliberate*, documented choice (the code comment explains it keeps the waveform silhouette's drawn fraction comparable across Recordings), so making it responsive is a real redesign question, not a one-line fix — worth its own follow-up ticket rather than folding into the accessibility ADR. Worth naming precisely because the setting lives in System Settings → **Appearance**, not **Accessibility** — Apple treats it as a general density preference that happens to help low-vision and motor-impaired users too, not as a disability-specific feature, which is the right frame for triaging its priority. |

## 6. The optimal solution for AppTape

Synthesizing §1–5 into a concrete rule set, phrased the way an ADR's "Consequences" section reads:

**Reduce Transparency (`@Environment(\.accessibilityReduceTransparency)`).** The two Liquid Glass controls (transport play/pause `.buttonStyle(.glass)`, Export `.buttonStyle(.glassProminent)`) need no conditional code — this is guaranteed by the `Glass` material's own contract, confirmed by Apple in the WWDC25 "Meet Liquid Glass" session, not an app decision to make or maintain. Nothing else in the editor uses translucency (the map already commits chrome to standard, non-blurred materials and content to flat fills), so there is no second surface to cover — the editor inherits the same rule the panel already applies (`Color(nsColor: .windowBackgroundColor)` when true), extended to any future chrome that happens to add a material. **Recommendation, going beyond documented Apple guarantees:** test both glass controls with Reduce Transparency on, on a real OS build, every OS cycle — Tahoe shipped with Reduce Transparency broken for several point releases (§7), so "the API promises this" and "this build actually does it" are not the same claim.

**Reduce Motion (`@Environment(\.accessibilityReduceMotion)`).** The two glass controls' own morph/elasticity need no app code (same WWDC guarantee). Everything the app itself animates does need a branch, and Apple's own stated preference is a fade, not a cut:
- The four existing unconditional `.animation(.default, value:)` calls in `ExportInspector.swift` (`recording.trimmedDuration`, `coordinator.phase`, `preference.normalizeLoudness`, `editor.correction.state`) should become `.animation(reduceMotion ? .linear(duration: 0.15) : .default, value: ...)` or equivalent — a short, non-bouncy cross-fade rather than the current spring, per the HIG's explicit "replacing transitions... with fades" and "tightening animation springs" bullets.
- **The playhead**, once built: its continuous sweep during active playback is *essential* motion — the primary way position is communicated — and should never be suppressed outright; Reduce Motion should instead flatten any spring/overshoot easing on it to linear. A click-to-seek jump, by contrast, is a discrete state change with a redundant textual time readout elsewhere in the transport — an instant reposition (no animation at all) is an acceptable, HIG-consistent simplification for Reduce Motion here; treat this specific call as my own judgment, since Apple's guidance names fades as the general preference but does not adjudicate this exact case.
- **The Trim region's dimming** (the excluded, trimmed-away portion of the waveform, presumably rendered at reduced opacity relative to the kept region): a static opacity difference is not itself an "animation" and is unaffected by Reduce Motion; only the *transition* into that dimmed state, if animated when a Trim handle moves, needs the same fade treatment as above.

**Increase Contrast (`@Environment(\.colorSchemeContrast)`).** Ship a High Contrast appearance variant of the Signal color set in the asset catalog (Any, Any-High-Contrast, Dark, Dark-High-Contrast) — this is the documented, zero-extra-code mechanism (`Color("Signal")` re-resolves automatically), and is best practice specifically because Signal is a committed, meaning-carrying brand accent, not an incidental tint. **My own recommendation, not found stated by Apple this specifically:** consider also strengthening the Trim region's dimming contrast (a higher opacity floor on the excluded region, or a firmer boundary stroke at the Trim handles) when `colorSchemeContrast == .increased`, since a subtle-by-design opacity difference is exactly the kind of low-contrast cue Increase Contrast exists to firm up, even though it isn't literally a foreground/background text-color pair the HIG's contrast-ratio table addresses directly.

**Differentiate Without Color (`@Environment(\.accessibilityDifferentiateWithoutColor)`).** Audit result: three of Signal's four intended uses are already structurally redundant with shape or position (the waveform's own silhouette carries the audio data regardless of color; the Trim region is bounded by literal handle positions; the playhead is a positioned line) and need no further change. The fourth — the selected Quality Preset rung in `ExportInspector.swift`'s `presetRow` — is currently color-only (tinted background fill + tinted number, no shape). **Concrete fix:** when this flag is true, add a small leading checkmark glyph (or a stroke border) to the selected row, matching the pattern the app already uses elsewhere for state (the Trim glyph and Seams glyph in `LibraryRow` are exactly this kind of redundant-shape cue). This should not be the default treatment for everyone — Apple's own Raycast/Bear-style restraint argument from the premium-treatment research still applies to the common case — only a supplementary cue gated on the accessibility flag, at zero cost to users who haven't asked for it.

Regarding ADR-0009's amber-only Runway tier specifically: **leave the design as-is, but word the ADR's acknowledgment more precisely than "a conscious trade."** State plainly that the HIG's own guidance ("convey information with more than color alone") is unconditional, that the amber tier does not meet it at the 3-hour mark, and that the mitigation is the bounded harm plus the guaranteed textual 30-minute follow-up — not a claim of compliance. **One concrete, low-cost improvement worth adding, going beyond what ADR-0009 currently does:** when `NSWorkspace.shared.accessibilityDisplayShouldDifferentiateWithoutColor` is true, render the amber tier's pre-rendered `NSImage` with a small `⚠` glyph added — the ADR's own stated reason for omitting it by default ("red and amber on one item reads as a rendering bug") is about the *common* case with two possible colors; it does not apply once the addition is conditioned specifically on a setting only colorblind/low-vision users have turned on. This is my own recommendation, not something ADR-0009 considered, and is offered as a follow-up rather than a rewrite of that ADR's core decision.

**The other settings:** Bold Text needs no code (§5 — not exposed to macOS users at all). Invert Colors needs no special handling (the waveform is a chart, not media, so the default system inversion is fine). Sidebar icon size is a real, verified gap in `LibraryRow`, but it's a density-preference gap, not an accessibility one, and its fixed-height row exists for a different, already-documented reason — file it as a separate, lower-priority ticket rather than folding it into this ADR.

**Environment keys the editor should read, summarized:**
- `@Environment(\.accessibilityReduceTransparency)` — only relevant if a future non-glass surface adds translucency; the two glass controls don't need it read at all.
- `@Environment(\.accessibilityReduceMotion)` — gate the four existing `.animation` calls and any future Trim/playhead transition.
- `@Environment(\.colorSchemeContrast)` — gate the Trim-dimming contrast boost, if adopted; Signal's High Contrast variant needs no environment read at all (the asset catalog handles it).
- `@Environment(\.accessibilityDifferentiateWithoutColor)` — gate the `presetRow` checkmark and, per the recommendation above, the amber Runway glyph (read via `NSWorkspace.shared.accessibilityDisplayShouldDifferentiateWithoutColor` in the AppKit-hosted menu bar code, the SwiftUI environment key inside the editor).

## 7. Precedent

**No citable case of a third-party shipping macOS app's specific handling of these settings under Liquid Glass was found — that is the honest answer, not padding.** The comparison apps from the earlier premium-treatment research (Things, Raycast, Bear, Craft, and the rest) were searched again for "Reduce Transparency," "Reduce Motion," and accessibility statements specifically; nothing citable came back beyond general accessibility marketing boilerplate, and no design write-up for any of them describes what their Liquid Glass adoption (where they have any) does under these settings.

**What *does* exist, and is worth citing precisely because it is not a flattering precedent, is Apple's own record with its own material.** Reduce Transparency shipped broken, by Apple's own system implementation, for two point releases of macOS Tahoe: "Reduce Transparency Does Not Work in macOS Tahoe 26.1?" and the same report recurring for 26.2, then "the freshly released macOS Tahoe 26.3 update has resolved an accessibility issue where the 'Reduce Transparency' feature was not working properly on the Mac." — [OS X Daily, 26.1](https://osxdaily.com/2025/11/04/reduce-transparency-does-not-work-in-macos-tahoe-26-1/), [OS X Daily, 26.3 fix](https://osxdaily.com/2026/02/13/reduce-transparency-works-again-in-macos-tahoe-26-3/). A more detailed independent write-up of the 26.3 fix confirms specific, concrete regressions and their repairs — "the sidebar header is now opaque," "the toolbar finally has a solid background when Reduce Transparency is turned on" (previously showing "a translucent gradient") — while noting the fix was incomplete ("toolbar buttons lack clear definition unless Increase Contrast is also activated," Siri still shows "illegible white text against a pastel background") and that fixing one Liquid Glass interaction (scroll-bar/column-divider overlap) introduced a new one (gaps between scroll bars and handles). — [Pixel Envy: MacOS Tahoe 26.3 Fixes a Few Bugs With 'Reduce Transparency'](https://pxlnv.com/linklog/tahoe-reduce-transparency/).

**The lesson this precedent actually teaches is not "don't trust the API contract" — it's "verify empirically, every cycle, because even Apple's own first-party implementation of its own material has shipped with this broken."** That is the practical justification for the "test on a real build" recommendation in §6, rather than treating the WWDC transcript's guarantee as something that can be verified once and never checked again.

## What I could not verify

Would require screenshotting a running Tahoe/macOS 27 build with each setting toggled, not further documentation search.
- **Whether SwiftUI's `Material` type (`.regularMaterial` etc.) is documented by Apple, in its own words, as automatically substituting an opaque fill under Reduce Transparency**, as distinct from the underlying `NSVisualEffectView`/`NSWorkspace.accessibilityDisplayShouldReduceTransparency` contract, which is worded as guidance to the app rather than an automatic-behavior guarantee. The behavior is well-corroborated by consistent secondary/community sources, but I did not find one Apple-authored sentence stating it as plainly as the Liquid Glass transcript does for glass.
- **Whether macOS's system-level "Invert colors" allows an app to exempt specific views the way iOS's Smart Invert does via `accessibilityIgnoresInvertColors(_:)`**, or whether it is a blunter whole-display inversion outside any per-app control on the Mac specifically. Not settled by any page found; low-priority for AppTape's actual content (a waveform, not media) but flagged rather than assumed.
- **Whether SF Symbols' indefinite effects (`.pulse`, `.variableColor`, and similar), such as the one the menu bar panel already gates manually with `!reduceMotion` for its record glyph, auto-honour Reduce Motion without an app-side check.** Community sources (not an Apple doc page found) state that standard symbol effects "automatically adjust or disable themselves" under Reduce Motion. If true, the panel's existing manual gate may be redundant-but-harmless defensive code rather than load-bearing; I could not confirm this against a primary Apple source, so I am not recommending its removal — only flagging that the manual check may not be strictly necessary for symbol effects specifically (as distinct from AppTape's own custom `.animation`/`withAnimation` calls, which unambiguously do need the manual check per §2).
- **Whether Apple's own dedicated Liquid Glass sessions (design labs, forum threads) said anything more specific about macOS-26/27-specific lensing behavior under Reduce Motion**, beyond the general "elastic properties" sentence in the "Meet Liquid Glass" transcript. The WWDC25 design-group-lab notes were located but not fetched in full; a deeper pass through those specific session Q&A transcripts could turn up more, if this needs to go further.
- **A visual, screenshot-based confirmation of any of the above**, consistent with the caveat stated up front — this entire research rests on written sources.
